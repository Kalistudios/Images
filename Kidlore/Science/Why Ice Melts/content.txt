The Secret Life of Melting Ice

Hot summer sunshine filled the backyard picnic while Lilly noticed her colorful ice cream melting quickly beside the lemonade cups. Watching the dripping ice made her curious about why ice melts. She looked at her older brother, Leo, who was already finished with his treat.

Lilly looked curiously at Leo and asked, “Why does cold ice melt into liquid water after sitting outside during warm sunny weather or even inside cozy warm rooms for some time?” She pointed to a stray ice cube on the table that was turning into a clear puddle.

Leo smiled warmly and explained, “Ice melts because heat energy from warm surroundings slowly changes solid frozen ice into liquid water when temperatures become high enough.” He held his hands out as if feeling the warmth of the air around them.

Lilly listened carefully before asking, “What is heat energy exactly?” Leo nodded proudly and explained that heat is energy making things warmer by increasing movement inside tiny particles. He told her to imagine everything is made of tiny, invisible bouncy balls.

Leo continued, “Inside frozen ice, water particles stay packed tightly together. Heat gives them energy, allowing particles to move freely and become liquid water.” He explained that as the sun warms the ice, those tiny balls start to dance and pull apart.

Lilly became curious and asked, “Why does ice melt faster during summer?” Leo smiled gently and explained that hotter weather transfers heat into ice more quickly than colder weather. The more heat there is, the faster the particles start to move.

Leo added, “That is why ice cubes melt quickly under the bright sunlight but stay frozen much longer inside cold freezers or snowy winter conditions.” He led her toward the kitchen to see where the ice stayed safe and solid.

Lilly looked very thoughtful and asked, “Can wind affect melting too?” Leo explained that warm moving air carries heat toward ice, helping it melt even faster sometimes. He blew a gentle puff of air toward her to show how air moves.

Leo continued, “Dark surfaces also absorb more sunlight and heat, causing ice cubes placed on black roads or metal tables to melt much more quickly afterward.” He showed her how the dark table felt warmer than the light-colored napkins.

Lilly asked curiously, “Does all frozen water melt at the same temperature?” Leo nodded wisely and explained that pure ice usually melts when temperatures reach zero degrees Celsius. He explained that this is the "magic number" for ice to start changing.

Leo smiled and explained, “People use melting ice for cooling drinks, preserving food, making delicious desserts, and helping injured body parts feel much less swollen or painful.” He pretended to hold an ice pack to his knee to show her.

Lilly became surprised and asked, “Can melted water freeze again later?” Leo nodded proudly and explained that water becomes ice again after losing heat inside very cold temperatures. It was like the particles decided to stop dancing and give each other a big hug.

Leo continued, “This amazing freezing and melting cycle happens naturally in giant glaciers, snow, lakes, oceans, and even inside refrigerators people use every single day.” He described the big, white mountains of ice in the far north.

Lilly happily understood that ice melts when heat energy changes frozen water into liquid water. She realized that temperature changes affect many different things around her everyday life, from her treats to the giant world outside.

As the golden sunset glowed across the peaceful backyard picnic, Leo gently reminded Lilly, “Science becomes exciting when simple everyday changes reveal amazing secrets about how the world works.” They sat together, watching the long shadows stretch over the grass.
